# OurStory
## The Apple II Special
The first time I touched a computer, it was an Apple II.
Not a lab machine. Not a corporate workstation. Not a beige box pretending to be important. A real Apple II. The kind of machine that felt alive. The screen glowed with a kind of intent. The keyboard had personality. The colors were bright, the prompts were direct, the whole experience felt like a handshake between imagination and electricity.
I was learning. I was building. I was writing little programs, trying to understand what made a machine think. It felt like discovering language itself. Every line of BASIC was a tiny act of creation. Every result was immediate. Every mistake taught me something. There is nothing sentimental about that memory; it is the foundation of who I became.
From there, I moved forward. The path got bigger, faster, stranger, and more complicated. I saw systems become more layered, more urgent, more interconnected. But the core lesson stayed with me: if you want to understand a machine, you must understand what it is actually doing. You do not get there by staring at abstract dashboards. You get there by looking at the structure, the data, and the rules that make a system behave the way it does.
That is where this project comes from.
## A Dream Bigger Than One Machine
I started with the Apple II because it gave me a sense that software was not just logic on a screen. It was a way to build worlds. It was a way to make machines respond to intent. It was proof that elegant systems could be approachable, playful, creative, and still deeply technical.
Then came the long climb into real systems engineering. More protocols. More layers. More complexity. More infrastructure. More software that had to work under pressure, not in a user-friendly demo. I began to see the Internet in a different way—not as a miracle or a vague abstraction, but as a huge, fragile machine made of standards, encoded messages, routing decisions, and protocol boundaries.
And one day I realized: the Internet is not broken because it is too big. It is broken because too much of the work happens in the invisible layers. We have grown accustomed to things failing silently or behaving unpredictably because the structure under them is too opaque. The fix is not more noise. The fix is clearer understanding.
So I wanted to build something that took the spirit of the Apple II—clarity, directness, hands-on learning—and applied it to the real problem of the modern internet: understanding data at the protocol level, parsing it thoughtfully, and refusing to pretend complexity is an excuse for fragility.
## The Artisanal ASN.1 Dream
This project is my answer to that dream.
It is an artisanal ASN.1 parser. Not a huge framework. Not a hypothetical platform. Not a glossy idea with no core. A focused parser. A real parser. A tool that understands structure, that respects binary rules, that reads encoded information and makes the hidden architecture visible.
There is something almost spiritual about that work. A stream of bytes looks meaningless until you know how to read it. Then it becomes a story. A sequence. An integer. A nested object. A decision point. A system speaking in its own language. That is the heart of protocol engineering. It is not glamorous. It is not lazy. It is exacting. It is honest.
And if I have learned anything from the Apple II, from the long arc of software engineering, and from the dream of fixing the internet, it is this: the systems that matter are the ones where we understand the structure beneath the abstraction.
We should not be intimidated by complexity. We should be disciplined enough to meet it. We should not pretend that structure is a burden. We should treat it as a craft.
This project is a small, real step toward that future.
A step from a bright little machine on a desk, to a larger dream of making the internet less opaque, more understandable, more resilient, and more worthy of the trust we place in it.
That is why this project matters.
That is why I keep going.
